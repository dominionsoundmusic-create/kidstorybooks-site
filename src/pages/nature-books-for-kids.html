Why children's books about nature matter

Children's books about nature feed a curiosity kids already have. Ask any four year old why the sky is blue or where bugs go at night and you will see it.

NAEYC, the national early childhood group, describes young children as active scientists from the day they are born, always exploring their world. But it also says being a born scientist does not mean a child can do it all alone. Adults help by giving them words, books and chances to look closely.

A good nature book does exactly that. It names what a child is seeing and shows the steps in order: egg, tadpole, froglet, frog. Then it sends them back outside to look for more.

Three kinds of nature books on this shelf

Life cycle books. These follow one living thing from start to finish: Lifecycle of a Frog, Lifecycle of a Honey Bee, Lifecycle of a Sea Turtle, Lifecycle of a Strawberry and more. Many have "Ages 3-5" in the title.

Nature stories with a lesson in the title. The Brave Bamboo: Learning to Bend in the Wind, The Honest Ivy: The Courage to Tell the Truth and The Remarkable Redwood: Standing Strong Together pair a plant with a character trait.

Spanish titles. El Ciclo de Vida del Salmón, El ciclo de vida de un pollo and El Pequeño Ciclo de Zumbido have Spanish titles.

Reading Rockets, a national literacy project, sorts science books for kids into several kinds, from narrative books that tell a story to hands-on books that teach a skill you can use, like a field guide. It also suggests information books with lots of colorful photos or pictures to help young children build knowledge about the world.

Take the book outside

The American Academy of Pediatrics says children play harder outdoors than indoors and need chances to play outside every day. It links more outdoor time with better motor skills and a lower risk of obesity and nearsightedness, and it notes studies that found children who spent more time exploring nature had better learning outcomes.

  • Wonder out loudNAEYC suggests modeling curiosity: "I wonder what would happen if we planted this seed in the shade?"
  • Do not rush to correctNAEYC also says not to correct a child's science ideas right away. Ask how they could find out instead.
  • Grow somethingA bean in a cup on the windowsill turns a plant life cycle book into something your child can watch day by day.
