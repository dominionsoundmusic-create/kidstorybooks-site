Why bedtime stories for kids are worth the time

Bedtime stories for kids do two jobs at once. They give your child time close to you, and they become a signal that sleep is next.

Sleep researchers call this a bedtime routine: the same calm steps, in the same order, every night in the hour before lights out. Reading a book is one of the most common steps, along with a bath and brushing teeth.

A 2018 review of studies on young children found that the more often families kept a routine like this, the better their children tended to sleep. The authors also think routines may help with bonding and with social and emotional growth, but they describe those as ideas still being studied, not proven facts.

Reading aloud helps in other ways too. Reach Out and Read, a national early literacy group that works through pediatricians, says children who are read to more often have stronger language and listening skills and stronger bonds with the people they love.

Brush, book, bed: a simple routine

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) suggests an easy order to remember: brush teeth, read a favorite book or two, then go to bed. Reading after brushing makes the story the last step before sleep.

  • Same time, same placeRead in the same spot each night, such as the bed or a favorite chair. Kids like knowing what comes next.
  • Let them chooseOffer two books and let your child pick. If they want the same one again, that is fine. Young children learn from hearing a story many times.
  • Keep the lights lowUse a soft lamp. Read in a quieter, slower voice as the story goes on.
  • Put screens awayThe AAP advises no screens in the hour before bed and keeping TVs, tablets and phones out of children's bedrooms.
  • Stop on timeAgree on how many books before you start. "Two books, then lights out" saves a lot of bargaining.
  • Talk a littleAsk a quiet question or two: "What was your favorite part?" Then let the talk fade so your child can settle.

How much sleep do preschoolers need?

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says children ages 3 to 5 need 10 to 13 hours of sleep in every 24 hours, counting naps. That is a lot more than most adults get, which is one reason an early, steady bedtime matters so much at this age.

Work back from the time your child needs to wake up. Then start the routine about half an hour before lights out so the story never feels rushed.

Short bedtime stories for busy nights

Some nights are long. On those nights, a short picture book still counts. Reading every night matters more than reading for a long time. If your child is very tired, read one book and save the second for tomorrow.
