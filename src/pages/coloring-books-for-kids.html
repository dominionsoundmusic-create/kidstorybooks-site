Why coloring books help preschool kids

Coloring books for preschool kids look simple, but every crayon stroke is practice. Small fingers learn to grip, press and steer, the same skills they will use to hold a pencil.

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) lists coloring books, crayons, markers and clay as gifts that build creativity and help improve fine motor skills. The AAP also notes research that strong fine motor skills in early childhood can predict not only writing success but better reading and math in elementary school.

ZERO TO THREE, a national early childhood group, says early art supports hand and finger control, problem solving and thinking in symbols.

What to expect by age

The CDC's milestone checklists list things most children (about three out of four) can do by a certain age. Every child is different, so use these as a rough guide, not a test.

  • By age 3Most children can draw a circle after you show them how. Big pages and chunky crayons work best.
  • By age 4Most children can draw a person with three or more body parts. Simple pictures with bold outlines are a good fit.
  • By age 5Most children can write some letters in their name. Coloring books with letters and numbers add practice.
  • Ages 6 and upKids can handle smaller spaces, more detail and puzzles that mix pictures and words.

Choosing a coloring book for your child

Match the theme. A child who loves trains will color a train for much longer than a random picture. Trains Coloring Book and The Unicorn Coloring Book are built around one favorite subject.

Plan for holidays. Christmas Coloring Book, Thanksgiving Coloring Book and St Patricks Day Coloring Book give kids something to do while the grown-ups cook.

See themselves. Bold & Brilliant: A Coloring Book for Black Girls says who it was made for right in the title.

Add words and numbers. Animal Crossword Puzzle and Fruit Word Puzzle are word puzzle books, as their titles say. Compte avec Moi : De Un à Dix has a French title about counting from one to ten.

Make it a together activity

Sit down and color a page next to your child. Talk about the colors you pick. Ask your child to tell you a story about their picture, then write it at the bottom for them. That small step connects coloring to reading and writing.
